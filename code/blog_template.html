London's blue plaques have been going up since 1867. I read every one listed on Wikipedia, {{eh.plaques|int}} plaques naming {{eh.people|int}} people, and mapped {{osm.objects|int}} plaques across the UK. For a century, about one person in ten honoured was a woman. Since 2020 it is more than half.

In 1867 the Society of Arts fixed a plaque to a house in King Street, St James's, where Napoleon III had lived in exile. It is still there, the oldest surviving plaque in London's scheme, and the scheme has kept going ever since: through the London County Council, the Greater London Council and, since 1986, English Heritage. Towns and civic societies all over Britain have copied it, in blue and in plenty of other colours.

A plaque is a small public verdict. Someone has decided that this person mattered, and that the street should be told so for the next hundred years. So I wanted to know who those verdicts have gone to. Men or women? Writers or engineers? London or everywhere else?

I used three open sources. Wikipedia's lists of English Heritage plaques in London give the year each plaque went up, and I parsed all {{eh.plaques|int}} rows. Wikidata holds {{openplaques.humans|int}} people with an Open Plaques ID, from schemes all over the country. And OpenStreetMap volunteers have mapped {{osm.objects|int}} plaques across the UK.

One in ten, then one in two

Of the {{eh.people|int}} people named on London's official plaques, {{eh.women|int}} are women. That is {{eh.share_women|pct0}}.

What surprised me was how flat the line was for so long. The London County Council, which ran the scheme from 1901 to 1964, honoured {{eh.eras.lcc.women|int}} women among {{eh.eras.lcc.people|int}} people ({{eh.eras.lcc.share_women|pct0}}). The Greater London Council, from 1965 to 1985, honoured {{eh.eras.glc.women|int}} among {{eh.eras.glc.people|int}} ({{eh.eras.glc.share_women|pct0}}). Then, very recently, the line turns: {{eh.share_2010s|pct0}} in the 2010s, {{eh.share_since2016|pct0}} since 2016, and {{eh.share_2020s|pct0}} in the 2020s so far, when {{eh.women_2020s|int}} of the {{eh.people_2020s|int}} people honoured have been women.

Wikipedia's history of the scheme says the first woman to get a plaque was the actor Sarah Siddons, in 1876; her house was demolished and the plaque taken down. The oldest surviving plaque to a woman in my data is Fanny Burney's, from {{eh.first_women.0.year|raw}}.

The same tilt shows up by birth date. Of the plaque people born in the 1700s, {{eh.by_birth_century.1.share|pct0}} are women; born in the 1800s, {{eh.by_birth_century.2.share|pct0}}; born in the 1900s, {{eh.by_birth_century.3.share|pct0}}. Across all the Open Plaques people in Wikidata, from every scheme, women are {{openplaques.share_women|pct0}}, very close to London.

Writers, painters and statesmen

I sorted each plaque by the first job its inscription mentions, so "Novelist lived here" counts as writing. Writers are the biggest group, with {{eh.groups_overall.Writing|int}} people, then artists, architects and designers ({{eh.groups_overall.Art and design|int}}), scientists and doctors ({{eh.groups_overall.Science and medicine|int}}) and politicians and lawyers ({{eh.groups_overall.Politics, law and public life|int}}).

The mix has moved. Writing fell from {{eh.eras.lcc.groups.Writing|pct0}} of those honoured by the London County Council to {{eh.eras.eh.groups.Writing|pct0}} under English Heritage, and politics and law from {{eh.eras.lcc.groups.Politics, law and public life|pct0}} to {{eh.eras.eh.groups.Politics, law and public life|pct0}}. Actors, musicians and sportspeople took their place: Freddie Mercury has a plaque in Feltham, and the first sporting plaque in my data went up in {{eh.sport_first_year|raw}}.

Women are {{eh.group_share_women.Campaigners and reformers|pct0}} of the campaigners and reformers and {{eh.group_share_women.Stage and screen|pct0}} of the stage and screen group, but {{eh.group_share_women.Engineering and invention|pct0}} of the engineers and inventors. Some of that is history: for much of the period these plaques cover, women were kept out of engineering, the professions and Parliament. Some of it is a choice about what counts as eminence.

London, and the places that beat it

Of the {{osm.objects|int}} mapped plaques, {{osm.london|int}} are in Greater London. London has {{osm.london_pop_share|pct0}} of the people of England and Wales but {{osm.london_share_of_ew|pct0}} of the plaques mapped there: {{osm.london_per_100k|f1}} per 100,000 residents, against {{osm.rest_ew_per_100k|f1}} in the rest. So the capital really is plaque-heavy.

But it is not the most plaqued place. Among the larger towns and cities, London comes {{osm.london_rank_bua|int}}th. Cheltenham has {{osm.top_bua.0.per_100k|f1}} mapped plaques per 100,000 people, York {{osm.top_bua.1.per_100k|f1}}, Oxford {{osm.top_bua.2.per_100k|f1}}, Bristol {{osm.top_bua.3.per_100k|f1}} and Brighton and Hove {{osm.top_bua.4.per_100k|f1}}. Bradford has {{osm.bottom_bua.4.plaques|int}} mapped plaques and Stoke-on-Trent {{osm.bottom_bua.5.plaques|int}}.

I would not read too much into the bottom of that list. These are mapped plaques, and mapping depends on volunteers. In York, {{osm.bua_york.linked|int}} of {{osm.bua_york.plaques|int}} mapped plaques are linked to the Open Plaques database; in Liverpool, {{osm.bua_liverpool.linked|int}} of {{osm.bua_liverpool.plaques|int}}. A low number can mean few plaques, or plaques nobody has mapped yet.

Not always blue

Only {{osm.colour_tagged|int}} mapped plaques have a colour recorded, so this is a sample. Just over half of them, {{osm.blue_share_tagged|pct0}}, are blue. But {{osm.colour_places.1.colours.green|int}} of {{osm.colour_places.1.n|int}} in Bristol are green, every one in Thetford and Wheathampstead is green, and in York all {{osm.colour_places.10.n|int}} are black.

Firsts, lasts and repeats

The oldest-born person with a London plaque of their own is Sir Harry Vane, born in {{eh.oldest_born_single.0.birth|raw}}. The youngest is Diana, Princess of Wales, born in {{eh.youngest_born.birth|raw}}, with a plaque from {{eh.youngest_born.year|raw}} on the flat in Earl's Court where she lived before her marriage. English Heritage asks that a person has been dead for {{context.rule_years_dead|int}} years, but the median plaque goes up {{eh.median_gap_all|int}} years after the death.

And some people get more than one. Across the UK, {{osm.multi_people|int}} people are linked to more than one mapped plaque. Charles Dickens leads with {{osm.multi_top.0.n|int}}, then John Logie Baird and Roald Dahl with {{osm.multi_top.1.n|int}} each.

How I did this

I parsed the wikitext of Wikipedia's list of English Heritage blue plaques in London and its borough lists for Camden, Kensington and Chelsea, and Westminster, taking the people, inscription, address and year of each plaque. I matched {{eh.matched_share|pct0}} of the people to Wikidata and took their recorded gender; for the rest I used titles such as Sir and Dame, then first names, and checked the last few by hand. Occupation comes from the first job word on the plaque. The UK-wide plaques are OpenStreetMap objects tagged as memorial plaques or linked to Open Plaques, placed in regions and towns with ONS small areas and set against Census 2021 populations.

The caveats matter. The Wikipedia lists cover surviving plaques, so the early decades are thin. Gender and occupation are inferred, so a few individual entries will be wrong, though not enough to move the shares. And Open Plaques and OpenStreetMap are volunteer records: they show what people have photographed and mapped, which differs a lot from town to town.
